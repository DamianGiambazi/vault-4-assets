
## Chapter 4

**1. The walk home**

Sarah was at the bus stop when they got off, though she usually took the earlier one, and she fell into step beside them as if they'd arranged it.

"The garden gnome man," she said. "Did you see? He's sent a new photo. The gnome in front of the Eiffel Tower. As evidence of theft."

"Evidence for whom?"

"For us. He wants it valued as well-travelled."

They laughed. It came out before they could stop it, a short, real sound, and Sarah looked pleased in a way she tried not to show.

At the postbox it began to rain. Sarah had an umbrella, a large, ugly green one, and she pushed it into their hands at the corner where their roads split. "Bring it tomorrow. I'm two minutes away." Then she was gone, her hood up, walking quickly.

They walked the last hundred metres under her umbrella, and something about the laugh stayed with them, like a step missed on a stair.

**2. The mat**

A gas statement. A leaflet. A charity envelope with a window. And a cream envelope, a little larger than standard, addressed in blue ink.

They knew the hand before they'd read the name of their own street in it: the capitals slightly too tall, the long downward stroke of the *y* reaching for the line below. They hadn't seen that *y* in fifteen years.

They picked it up last, by the edges. On the back, where a return address would go, there was only one line in the same hand:

*Please, this time.*

**3. The hall**

They stood there with their coat off one shoulder until the weight of it pulled at the other arm. When they moved, they put the keys on the bare shelf beside the door, not in the blue dish beside them. Sarah's umbrella dripped into the stand.

**4–5. The kitchen; dinner**

The kitchen faced the street. They laid the envelope face down at the far end of the table, in front of the chair nobody used, and squared it to the edge. The gas statement went into the second drawer, behind the divider marked *Utilities*.

They measured the rice with a cup and levelled it with a knife, though they had poured it by eye for years. They diced the onion evenly and cut again the pieces that weren't. They didn't put the radio on. They wiped the counter, then wiped it in the other direction.

While they ate, the gnome came back to them, the Eiffel Tower, Sarah's face, and they waited to smile, and didn't.

**6–7. The window; the table**

At eight, the window at number thirty-one stayed dark. They went through the reasons: an evening out, illness, a holiday, a move. At twenty past they found they were standing.

They sat back down. On one side, a dark window. At the end of the table, the envelope.

Something surfaced: another kitchen, another table, a chair pulled out from it and left that way. They got up and pushed their own chair in.

**8. The cup**

They were drying up when they knocked the cup against the tap.

It was a small sound. A line ran from the rim halfway down the side, fine as a hair. They held it up to the light and turned it. The handle was sound and the base was sound. The question was whether it would hold liquid, and for how long, and whether the crack would spread under heat, and they stood there with the tea towel over one arm and found they couldn't decide. They had decided on far more complicated things every day for twenty years. They couldn't decide about a cup.

They put it down on the counter, too hard, and it came apart in three pieces.

The sound that came out of them wasn't a word. It surprised them; they hadn't heard their own voice since the bus stop.

Then the other cups went off the rack. They didn't so much decide to do it as find their arm already moving. The cups hit the tiles one after another, a noise far bigger than the cups had ever seemed, and they stood in it as if someone else had done it.

Then the drawer was open. They didn't remember opening it. The envelope was in their hands, their thumbs at the edge, and the paper had begun to give, a short clean tear, a centimetre, less, and they stopped, because the sound of that was worse than the cups.

They sat down where they were. The floor was cold. There was a piece of handle by their foot. What came up out of them then wasn't crying at first. It was more like a cough that wouldn't finish. Then it was crying. It was graceless and loud and it went on for a long time, and their face did whatever it wanted.

The light was on. The blind was up.

**9–10. The knock**

They had the pieces swept into a pile and were wrapping them in newspaper when the knock came.

They stayed where they were, kneeling, holding a parcel of broken cup. The newspaper was damp where their hands had been. The drawer was still open. The envelope lay on the table with its small tear showing, and they hadn't yet looked at their face.

The knock came again, and then her voice through the door: "It's only me. Sorry — it's raining again, and it's my only one."

The umbrella.

They could stay still. The hall was dark. She would think they were out, or in the bath, or asleep, and she'd walk home wet, and tomorrow they'd hand it back at work and apologise and nothing would have happened.

They got up.

**11. The door**

They opened it halfway and stood in the gap.

Sarah was on the step with her hood up and rain on her glasses. She looked at their face, and she didn't pretend not to. That was the first thing. She didn't let her eyes go politely to the umbrella stand.

"I saw your light," she said.

They had a sentence ready. It was a good one: *I dropped something — it's fine — I'm just tired*. It was the kind of sentence they'd written a thousand times in notes fields, true enough and closing the matter. They opened their mouth to say it.

"I got a letter today," they said. "From someone I haven't heard from in fifteen years."

Rain ticked on the porch roof. Somewhere down the street a car door shut.

Sarah didn't ask who. She didn't ask what was in it, or step forward, or reach for their arm. She stood on the step, wet, and considered them seriously, as if they had said something perfectly reasonable.

"Have you read it?"

"No."

She nodded. Just once, as if that were an answer a person might reasonably give.

They handed her the umbrella. She took it, and for a moment neither of them moved.

"Okay," Sarah said. "Tomorrow, then." And she went down the path and through the gate, closing it carefully so it didn't knock, the way they always did. They didn't know she'd noticed that.

**12. After**

They shut the door and leaned on it, and the wood was cold against their back.

Then they finished. They wrapped the rest of the pieces and put the parcels in the bin. They wiped the floor. They took the envelope from the table, smoothed the tear flat with the side of their thumb, and put it in the drawer under the boiler warranty, and closed the drawer until it clicked. They went to the hall, took the keys from the shelf and put them in the dish.

The kitchen looked exactly as it always did, except there were no cups in it.

They turned off the light at half past nine, which was early.

**13. Midnight**

They didn't sleep. Some time after midnight they went downstairs in the dark, hand on the banister, and stopped in the kitchen doorway.

The window at number thirty-one was lit, low, as if by a lamp in another room. By the curtain there was a shape that might have been a person standing or a coat on a hook, or only the fold of the green fabric.

They looked at it for a long time. Then they looked at their own window, the blind still up, and at the street lamp outside, newer and whiter than the one across the street, shining straight in.

All those evenings, they'd thought the glass only went one way.

They didn't lower the blind. They stood in the dark doorway of their own kitchen, and for the first time in fifteen years they knew for certain they had been seen, and they stayed there a while longer, letting it be true.
